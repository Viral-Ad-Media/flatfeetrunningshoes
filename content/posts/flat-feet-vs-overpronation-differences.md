People often use "flat feet" and "overpronation" as if they mean the same thing. They're related, but they aren't the same, and mixing them up can lead you to the wrong shoe.

The short version: **flat feet are a shape. Overpronation is a movement.**

## What flat feet are

A flat foot has a low or collapsed medial arch, so most or all of the sole touches the ground when you stand. Many people have flat feet their whole lives without any trouble. Others develop them over time through tendon wear, weight gain, pregnancy or injury.

There are two broad types:

- **Flexible flat feet.** The arch appears when you sit or stand on your toes and disappears when you stand normally. This is the most common type and usually isn't painful.
- **Rigid flat feet.** The arch stays flat no matter what. This is less common and worth getting checked by a doctor or podiatrist.

## What overpronation is

Pronation is the natural inward roll of your foot after your heel lands. It's how your foot absorbs shock, so some pronation is a good thing.

Overpronation means the foot keeps rolling inward further, or for longer, than it can comfortably control. The ankle drops inward, the arch flattens more under load, and the lower leg rotates in. Over many miles that extra motion can add stress to the shins, knees and the tissues under the foot.

## How they're connected

Flat feet and overpronation often show up together, because a low arch gives the foot less natural structure to resist rolling in. But:

- **Not everyone with flat feet overpronates.** Plenty of flat-footed runners have a stable, efficient stride.
- **Not everyone who overpronates has flat feet.** People with normal arches can overpronate because of weak hips, tight calves or fatigue late in a run.

That's why looking at your arch alone isn't enough to choose a shoe.

## How to tell which you have

1. **The wet footprint test.** Wet your foot and stand on a piece of paper or cardboard. A print that shows almost the whole sole suggests a low arch. It tells you about shape, not motion.
2. **Check your old shoes.** Heavy wear along the inside edge of the forefoot and heels that tilt inward when the shoes sit on a table hint at overpronation.
3. **Film yourself running.** Record from behind on a treadmill at an easy pace. If your ankles collapse visibly inward after each landing, you probably overpronate.
4. **Get a gait analysis.** Many running stores and physiotherapists offer one. It's the most reliable way to see what your feet actually do.

## What it means for your shoes

- **Flat feet, no overpronation, no pain:** a comfortable neutral shoe with a stable base is often fine. You don't need to "fix" a foot that isn't causing problems.
- **Flat feet with overpronation:** a [stability running shoe](/running-shoes-for-flat-feet-stability-2026-guide/) with guide rails, a firmer medial side or a wider base can help control the extra roll.
- **Pain or recurring injuries:** consider [arch support insoles](/arch-support-for-flat-feet-what/) and see a podiatrist, especially if your arch is rigid.

## The bottom line

Your arch height tells you what your foot looks like. Your gait tells you what it does. Choose shoes based on both, and on what feels comfortable when you run, rather than on the footprint test alone.
